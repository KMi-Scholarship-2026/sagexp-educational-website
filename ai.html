History of AI

Artificial intelligence (AI) developed from the idea that computers could perform tasks associated with aspects of human intelligence, such as reasoning, problem-solving and decision-making. Early AI systems often relied on rules written by people. These systems could work well when problems were clearly defined, but could struggle when situations did not fit the rules provided.

  • 1950: Alan Turing explored whether machines could demonstrate intelligent behaviour in Computing Machinery and Intelligence.
    • He proposed the Turing Test as a way of considering machine intelligence.
  • 1956: The Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence helped establish AI as a formal research area and the term “Artificial Intelligence” became associated with this work.
  • 1966: Joseph Weizenbaum creates ELIZA, an early conversational chatbot
  • 1970s: Interest and funding for AI crashed because computers back then weren't powerful enough. This period is known as the first "AI Winter."
  • 1980s: AI made a return using specialised software (called “expert systems”) that acted like a digital consultant for specific, niche tasks.
    • AI has continued to expand into language, image recognition, decision-making and wearable technology. Modern AI research also places increasing emphasis on evaluating how capable, reliable and responsible AI systems are.

Types of AI

There are different types of AI, which can be categorised based on their capabilities and applications. The main types include:

  • Artificial Narrow Intelligence: AI designed to perform a particular task or limited set of tasks.
    • Examples include recommendation systems, image-recognition systems and classification systems.
    • SAGEXP: the wearable stress-classification component is most closely described as narrow AI because it has a specific purpose.
  • Artificial General Intelligence Form of AI able to perform a broad range of intellectual tasks rather than being limited to one application.
  • Artificial Superintelligence: Form of AI that would exceed human intellectual capabilities across a broad range of tasks.

AI vs Machine Learning

Artificial intelligence is the broader term for computer systems designed to carry out tasks associated with human intelligence, including reasoning, learning, planning, perception and communication.

  • Does not have to learn from data. Some AI systems can rely on rules, logic or other explicitly designed methods.
Machine learning (ML) is a subset of AI. Rather than requiring a programmer to write every rule, a machine-learning model learns patterns or relationships from examples in data.
  • AI and ML are related but aren not interchangeable terms
    • Machine learning is one approach that can be used to build AI systems.
For SAGEXP, the wearable's classifier uses machine learning to analyse physiological information, while the wider system also includes an AI-based explanation component.

Real-world AI applications (SAGEXP)

AI is used in applications including recognising patterns in images, understanding language, making recommendations and supporting decision-making. In SAGEXP, AI-related technologies have different roles rather than performing the same job.

  • Wearable device: collects physiological data and uses a machine-learning classifier to determine whether the measurements are indicative of a stress response.
  • Generative AI Explanation Assistant: receives the classification result and relevant model information and uses the educational website as a knowledge base when generating explanations.
  • Educational Website: provides accessible background information about AI, machine learning, stress detection and explainable AI.
The wearable performs sensing and classification, while the explanation assistant is responsible for communicating and explaining the result.
