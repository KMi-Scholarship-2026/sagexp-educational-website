AI and Stress Detection

Stress detection involves identifying physiological changes in the body that may be associated with a person's stress response, using signals such as heart activity, skin conductance, breathing and body temperature.

Stress Detection

Stress Detection – identifying stress-related states by measuring changes in the body that can occur during a stress response.

  • When a person experiences a stressor, physiological systems can change.
  • These changes can include alterations in heart activity, sweating, breathing, temperature and muscle activity.
Wearable stress-detection research began exploring whether these physiological changes could be detected outside clinical settings in the early 2000s.

Research has increasingly considered individual differences because people can respond differently to the same situation.
  • A key limitation is that physiological responses are not unique to stress.
  • For example, increased heart activity can occur during exercise as well as emotional stress.
A wearable is therefore detecting physiological patterns associated with stress rather than directly measuring a person's thoughts or feelings

AI for stress detection

AI and machine-learning methods can analyse physiological measurements and identify patterns associated with stress.

  • A typical approach can involve collecting physiological signals, cleaning raw measurements, calculating useful features, training a model and evaluating its predictions.
  • The model can learn from labelled examples and then use learned patterns to classify new measurements.
  • The quality of the input data matters. Noisy signals, movement and differences between individuals can make the task more difficult.
  • Traditional machine-learning approaches have been widely used in wearable stress-detection research, particularly where models need to work with physiological features.
  • A model's accuracy should not be considered on its own.
    • It is also important to consider whether it works on new people or real-world situations and whether its predictions can be appropriately interpreted

Physiological Signals

Physiological – Relating to the normal physical functions of living organisms and their parts. It describes bodily processes and reactions

  • Stress can affect several physiological systems.
  • Different signals provide different types of information, so combining several signals can sometimes give a fuller picture than relying on one signal alone
There are many physiological signs that can be used to identify stress, including (but not limited to):
  • Electrodermal Activity (EDA)
  • Photoplethysmogram (PPG)
  • Electrocardiogram (ECG)
  • Body Temperature
SAGEXP uses all 4 of these signs in the wearable to identify stress in the user

Electrodermal Activity (EDA) – Measures changes in the skin's electrical conductance.
  • When sweat glands become more active, the skin can conduct electricity differently.
  • Because sweat-gland activity is influenced by the sympathetic nervous system, EDA can provide information about physiological arousal associated with stress.
Photoplethysmogram (PPG) – An optical technique that uses light to measure changes in blood volume beneath the skin.
  • Wearable devices can use PPG to obtain cardiovascular information, including heart rate.
  • Useful for wearables because it can be measured at locations such as the wrist, although movement can affect the signal.
Electrocardiogram (ECG) – Records the electrical activity of the heart using electrodes.
  • Can provide information about heart activity and measures such as heart rate and changes in the timing between heartbeats.
  • Wearable ECG measurements can be affected by movement and electrode placement.
Body Temperature – Regulated by the body and can change as a result of physiological processes.
  • Can provide additional information when studying changes in the body's response, but it is influenced by many factors and should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of stress.

Wearable AI for stress detection

Wearable devices can collect physiological signals continuously and non-invasively while a person goes about normal activities.

  • The Process
    1. Signal collection: sensors collect physiological measurements from the body.
    2. Signal cleaning: measurements may contain noise or artefacts caused by movement, the sensor or the surrounding environment, so preprocessing is often required.
    3. Feature extraction: useful characteristics are calculated from processed signals so the model can use them.
    4. Classification: the trained model analyses the features and produces a prediction about the stress-related category.
  • Using multiple signals can be useful because no single physiological measurement provides a complete picture of stress.

Limitations of AI for Stress Detection

  • A model that performs well in a controlled experiment may not perform equally well in everyday life.
    • Real-world data can contain more movement, noise and variation between people.
  • Establishing the correct stress label, or 'ground truth', can be difficult because stress is subjective and physiological responses can have several possible causes.
